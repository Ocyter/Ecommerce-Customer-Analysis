CASE STUDY 02 · CONVERSION ANALYSIS

The Cart-to-Purchase Gap

I compared customers who bought with those who didn’t to see how much recorded cart activity differed.

500 CUSTOMERS401 PURCHASERS99 NON-PURCHASERS95 CART USERS AMONG NON-PURCHASERS

THE FINDING

95 of 99

customers who didn’t buy had recorded at least one cart addition.

Based on the same 500-customer portfolio dataset.

METHOD & EVIDENCE

Compare outcomes without inventing a journey.

  1. Create the flags. Define purchase activity as Total_Purchases > 0 and cart activity as Items_Added_to_Cart > 0.
  2. Cross-check the groups. Count non-purchasers with cart activity and purchasers with no recorded cart additions.
  3. Compare distributions. Inspect counts, means, medians and the cart/purchase cross-tab rather than relying on one headline average.
  4. Limit the conclusion. Similar averages alone are not a predictive-model test. Event timestamps and checkout steps would be needed to investigate actual abandonment.

Data context

An audit reproduced the buyer counts, cart cross-tab and location purchase rankings on a public comparison file. Some browsing, page and cart averages differ from the original report. The historical file, its origin and reuse terms remain unverified. See the source comparison and discrepancies.

Interpret these as descriptive portfolio findings, not audited company results or an improvement delivered for a client.

THE BUSINESS QUESTION

Does cart activity actually tell us who will buy?

A generic funnel might stop at “customers add to cart before purchasing.” This analysis focuses on the customers who break that expected path.

THE EVIDENCE

THE CONTRADICTION

Cart activity was similar across both groups.

Average cart additions are remarkably similar between purchasers and non-purchasers. That makes cart activity more useful as an engagement signal than a clear way to distinguish the two groups in this dataset.

01

95 non-buyers had carts

Most customers who did not purchase still recorded at least one cart addition.

02

37 buyers had zero carts

Some purchasing customers did not follow the expected cart-before-purchase path in the recorded data.

03

5.17 vs 5.06

Average cart additions differ very little between purchasers and non-purchasers.

04

Do not overclaim

Without event timestamps or checkout steps, the data cannot explain why individual customers abandoned or converted.

WHAT TO DO NEXT

Find the missing journey.

01

Instrument event-level sessions so cart additions can be ordered against product views, checkout starts and purchases.

02

Segment the 95 cart-active non-purchasers to identify whether they differ by device, location or browsing intensity.

03

Investigate the 37 zero-cart purchasers because their journey may bypass the simplified funnel entirely.

04

Do not treat cart count alone as a conversion score until richer behavioural data validates it.
