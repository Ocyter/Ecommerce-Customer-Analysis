CASE STUDY 01 · CUSTOMER ANALYTICS

What separates browsing from buying?

I analysed 500 customer records to see how purchases varied by device, location and customer profile, and whether browsing and cart activity tracked with purchase count.

METHOD & EVIDENCE

How the analysis was approached.

  1. Inspect the structure. The Python/pandas script loads the CSV, prints its dimensions and types, and describes numeric fields.
  2. Define the measures. Purchase rate is the proportion with Total_Purchases > 0. Average purchases includes all customers in each group, including non-buyers.
  3. Compare the groups. Group by device, gender and location; compare means and calculate Pearson correlations for numeric variables.
  4. Keep the limits visible. Customer-level counts do not reveal session order, causal effects or predictive accuracy. The comparison file has 500 distinct customer IDs, no missing values and no duplicate rows.

The web tables are static HTML summaries of the published report. They are not a Power BI report or a live database connection. The audit used a publicly accessible comparison file; the original file was unavailable.

WHAT I FOUND

Browsing metrics tell only part of the story.

Mobile was the largest group.

Mobile accounted for 178 customers and averaged 2.53 purchases per customer. The differences across devices were modest.

Delhi led on both measures.

It had the highest purchase rate (87%) and average purchase count (2.73) in the sample.

Browsing and cart signals were weak.

Browsing time, pages viewed and cart items all had very weak linear relationships with the number of purchases.

WHAT I’D INVESTIGATE NEXT

Where do customers stop?

I’d collect timestamps for product views, cart additions, checkout steps and purchases, then compare where sessions end across devices. That would show which stages deserve a closer look.
